# PrankDial

PrankDial is a consumer entertainment product built around prerecorded call scenarios. My work on the product was part of my Lead Developer role at Kickback Apps, where I spent a substantial part of my work programming Asterisk call trees alongside maintaining prankdial.com and its Express.js API during the January 2019–December 2021 engagement recorded in my [career account](/archive/kickback-apps/).

The product's public history provides context for that work: an established web service with a mobile app, a catalog of audio content, and a token-based purchase model.

## My contribution

A substantial part of my PrankDial work was Asterisk call-tree programming: working on the call-flow logic behind the product's telephone interactions. This placed my contribution within the telephony system as well as the web application.

Alongside that work, I maintained the PrankDial website and its Express.js API as part of the wider Kickback consumer-app portfolio. My responsibilities spanned the user-facing website, its backend interface, and the programming of call behavior.

The broader role also covered media workflows for recordings and a migration to stateless, serverless infrastructure that reduced AWS operating costs. Those are portfolio-level responsibilities in my career record; the available account does not assign every media feature or infrastructure change specifically to PrankDial.

This was work on an existing product with an established history. The mobile screens below are described as product context, rather than as a claim that I designed or implemented the iOS application.

## Product experience

The [App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/prankdial-1-prank-call-app/id367287593) identifies KickBack, Inc as the developer and describes scenario selection, automated calls, saved call history, and comments. It lists the app as free with in-app purchases, including token packages.

The four promotional screenshots supplied for this record show how the mobile experience connected those ideas. Their capture date is unknown, so the descriptions document the supplied interface rather than promise current feature availability.

### Browse and preview

The catalog screen combines category filters, popular and newest views, illustrated scenario cards, and audio previews. A token balance remains visible above the catalog. Individual cards show usage and feedback indicators, with labels identifying new or customizable content.

This presentation makes the audio catalog the starting point of the experience. A visitor can compare scenarios and preview the material before moving further into the call workflow.

### Customize a scenario

The customization screen presents editable fields within a script, including names and other scenario details. It shows the additional token cost next to the explanation and provides a distinct action for saving the changes.

The interface gives customization a defined place between content selection and execution. The user sees which parts of the scenario can change and the cost associated with that choice.

### Follow call progress

The live status screen keeps the selected scenario visible alongside a call-state label, recipient display, elapsed time, and an audio control. It gives the user feedback while the call is underway.

From a product perspective, that screen addresses a different need from browsing: explaining the state of an operation after it has started. The screenshot demonstrates the presentation, without revealing the underlying telephony implementation.

### Return to history and recordings

The history screen pairs earlier calls with timestamps, scenario labels, and playback controls. An expanded recording includes a progress slider and duration display. Persistent navigation connects history with browsing, tokens, reactions, and account settings.

The result is a product flow that extends beyond initiating a call to reviewing earlier activity and replaying recordings.

## Origins and commercial context

In an [October 2018 founder essay](https://medium.datadriveninvestor.com/what-i-learned-from-turning-a-prank-calling-passion-project-into-a-multi-million-dollar-product-1486b4cda535), Fahim Saleh described starting PrankDial as a website using recorded audio, then introducing advertising and paid tokens. He also described bringing in a developer for the iOS app while he worked on the backend.

Saleh reported more than $10 million in lifetime revenue at the time of writing. This is a historical, self-reported company figure from before my documented Kickback engagement, not an outcome attributed to my work. The essay is useful context for understanding the established product I later helped maintain.

## What the work represents

PrankDial sits within the consumer-product side of my career: programming Asterisk call trees and maintaining a website and API within a portfolio with ongoing delivery and infrastructure needs. The public interface adds context to that engineering work by showing the range of experiences around a seemingly simple interaction—content discovery, customization, progress feedback, and recorded history.
